The hardware used to run a water polo match, the goals that define the field of
play, the game and shot clocks that govern possession, and the displays and table
gear that officials use to manage a period. Water polo is a timed team game, so its
equipment centres on two clocks running at once: the game clock and the shot clock.
This overview follows the World Aquatics rules; other bodies may set different figures.

## What lives here

Water polo equipment is grouped by role:

- Goals, the floating frames and nets set at each end of the field of play.
- Game and shot-clock consoles, the timing console at the officials' table that
  runs the period clock and the possession (shot) clock together.
- Deck displays, the shot-clock and game-clock faces mounted at each end of the
  pool where players and referees can read them.
- Scoreboards, the boards showing the score, period, and time, often integrated
  with the pool's timing system.
- Table equipment, the exclusion and possession indicators and other gear the
  table officials use to manage fouls and the running score.

## Field of play and goals

World Aquatics sets the length of play, goal line to goal line, at 20 to 30 m for men and 20
to 25 m for women, with a width of 10 to 20 m and water at least 1.80 m deep
throughout. The federation running a match may allow exceptions to the field
dimensions.[^fr]

The goal has 3.0 m between the inner faces of its posts, and the underside of the crossbar
sits 0.9 m above the water. Posts and crossbar are white, with 80 mm rectangular sections, and
may be wood, metal or plastic. A limp net encloses the goal, leaving at least 0.3 m clear
behind the goal line. Markers along both sides show the goal lines, the 2 m and 6 m lines and
the half-way point, and each excluded players' re-entry area measures 2.0 by 1.08 m.[^fr]

## Game clock and shot clock

A match has four periods of eight minutes of actual play. The game clock stops at every
stoppage and restarts when the ball is back in play, so a period lasts longer than eight
minutes of elapsed time. The intervals are two minutes after the first and third periods and five
minutes at half-time.[^wacr]

The shot clock limits possession. A team has 28 seconds to shoot, and failing that the other
team receives an ordinary foul. The clock resets on a change of possession or a shot, and a
goal returns it to 28 seconds. A team that recovers its own rebound has 18 seconds, as it does
after some restarts.[^wacr] The time need only be kept with a reliable device, but the rules
call for one or more clocks that officials and players can see.[^wacr]

In practice the [water polo console](console/index.md) runs both clocks, and
[water polo shot clocks](shot-clock/index.md) at the ends of the pool display them. The
shot-clock overview covers how the displays are wired, their electrical siting, and the models
on sale. Older equipment and documents may show 30 and 20 seconds, the possession times of
their day.[^calypsods]

## Officials' table

Two timekeepers and two secretaries sit at the officials' table, which stands behind the
referees' platform on the side of the assistant referees, or farther from the pool if the
organizers choose. The officials there must be able to see the whole field.[^wacr] One
timekeeper records actual play, timeouts and intervals, the other records possession, and
the timekeepers operate the shot clock. Their whistle marks 45 seconds into a timeout, a
timeout's end and a period's end.[^wacr]

The secretaries keep the match record and manage exclusions. Flags, or another approved
signal, tell the pool what has happened: the appropriate flag marks the end of a player's
exclusion, and a red flag marks an improper entry or a third personal foul.[^wacr] Referee platforms run
along both sides, 1 m wide and 70 cm above the water.[^fr]
