The OSM-5 is a portable swim timing console made by Omega Electronics, with a printer built
into its carrying case. It printed every lane's splits and finishes as the race went on, from
touchpads or from timers' pushbuttons.[^sw7510][^sw7601] Omega's North American representative
introduced it in late 1975 as a low-cost touchpad system for six, eight or ten lanes, and it was
advertised in the United States until 1982.[^sw7510][^st8211] The [OSM6](osm6.md) replaced it,
and Omega's swim timing equipment is now made by [Swiss Timing](../../../vendors/swiss-timing.md).

## Models and naming

This section places the OSM-5 among the Omega consoles sold in North America.

The OSM numbers belong to a family of Omega swim timing systems. In 1974 Omega's North American
representative advertised two of them side by side. The OSM-3 was a fixed installation with an
operator console and a separate computer cabinet that printed every split and finish. The
[OSM-4](osm-4.md) was a portable console for six to ten lanes, which needed a separate digital
printer.[^sw7412] The OSM-4 had been shown for the first time at the 1973 Canada Games, where
the organisers bought the demonstration unit.[^sw7309] The [OSM-3](osm-3.md) was still in use at
the Etobicoke Olympium in Toronto in 1977.[^sn33] The OSM-5 combined the portable console and
the printer in one case.[^sw7510]

Omega's earlier timing equipment had been sold under the
[Swim-O-Matic](swim-o-matic.md) name, and the OSM6 still printed that name on its
results.[^sw7305]
No source says whether the OSM-5 did the same.

Complete systems were sold under the item numbers OSM-5 (6)-A, (8)-A and (10)-A, for six, eight
and ten lanes.[^sw7601] A six-lane semi-automatic system, with pushbuttons instead of
touchpads, was offered from 1976.[^sw7603]

## Role in the timing system

This section describes what an OSM-5 installation contained; the general background on timing
consoles is on the [timers overview](index.md).

A 1976 system comprised the console, [OCP-5](../touchpad/ocp5.md) touchpads measuring 5 ft by
2 ft with storage cases, a starting pistol with a transducer and cable, a deck cable harness,
and operation and maintenance manuals.[^sw7601] Options were one, two or three pushbuttons per
lane, the larger 6 ft 6 in Omega pads, and display boards for spectators.[^sw7601] By 1978 a
horn start with a public address system was also offered, and Omega relay judging units could
be mounted on the starting blocks.[^sw7809] In 1978 Seagull chose
[Daktronics](../../../vendors/daktronics.md) as a supplier of low-cost scoreboards to work with
Omega systems.[^sn46]

## History

This section traces the console's sales, distributors and use.

[Seagull Enterprises](../../../vendors/seagull.md), of Los Altos, California, was Omega's
exclusive representative in the United States and Canada and announced the OSM-5 in October
1975. Complete American systems started at $5,500, and Canadian ones at $7,700.[^sw7510][^sn18]
In 1976 Seagull added a six-lane semi-automatic system at $3,700.[^sw7603] Its 1976 staff
advertisement showed an OSM-5 at the Long Beach Olympic Trials.[^sw7607] Later advertising
claimed that the whole system would fit in a station wagon, and quoted a national high
school coach of the year who had chosen it after testing the touchpad.[^sw7701][^sw7709]

By August 1978 Seagull said it had installed 28 Omega swim timing systems in Canada, including
an OSM-5 with an eight-lane scoreboard ordered by the Crystal Pool in Victoria and a portable
OSM-5 at Simon Fraser University.[^sn46] It also said that an OSM-5 was in use at the world
championships in Berlin that month.[^sn46] A Colorado Time Systems advertisement in January
1979 gave Omega's prices from a September 1978 price sheet as $7,700, $9,300 and $11,200 for
six, eight and ten lanes. Those figures are a competitor's, and they are well above Seagull's
1976 prices for the same three packages.[^sw7901][^sw7601]

Seagull's Omega advertising stops in early 1979. From late 1979 the console was sold by Omega's
own American sales office in Hatboro, Pennsylvania, which advertised it until November 1982 as
a portable, fully automatic console for up to ten lanes that could drive scoreboards of one to
ten lines, with a one-year warranty and rental available.[^sw7911][^sw8008][^st8211] From
1980 the same Hatboro address and telephone number were listed for
[Sportyme](../../../vendors/sportyme.md), Omega's later agent for the OSM6.[^sw7911][^sw8005]

In Canada the OSM-5 timed the 1981 Canada Games in Thunder Bay, with a horn start and an
eight-lane board, and club meets such as a 1981 invitational in Saskatoon.[^sn73][^sn70] At the
1982 Commonwealth Games in Brisbane it was used with Omega's
[OSB-3](../relay-judging/osb-3.md) starting blocks, which sensed relay take-offs. The FINA
secretary said afterwards that FINA had not been asked to approve that equipment. Omega's
Canadian representative said nobody knew how long the block took to register a swimmer
leaving it, or how long a touchpad took to register a squeeze. The Canadian team manager
complained that officials dropped the video check after the first days and judged relay
exchanges on the block timing alone.[^sn87] Swiss Timing later dated Omega's introduction of
automatic relay take-off detection to the 1984 Olympic Games, with the OSM6 and the OSB6 blocks,
although the OSB-3 had served that purpose in Brisbane two years earlier. FINA allowed the 1984
equipment to be called "FINA Approved" in 1985.[^relay][^sw8511]

Used systems changed hands into the 1980s and 1990s. A seller in Pleasanton, California offered an eight-lane
OSM-5 with a horn start for $6,400 in 1982.[^sw8206] In 1994 the Crystal Pool in Victoria sold
its own system, with ten touchpads, an OSM 5 with a multiplexer and a Daktronics eight-lane
board.[^sn197]

## Design and hardware

This section collects what the advertising says about the console itself.

The console is built into a briefcase-style carrying case, with the printer and the controls
on one panel.[^sw7510][^sw8008] Omega's distributor gave its weight as about 10 lb.[^sw7809] It
takes inputs from touchpads for automatic timing or from pushbuttons for semi-automatic timing,
counts laps by itself, and lets the operator set the delay before a lane's pad is armed again
after a touch.[^sw7601][^sw7809] Omega's 1980 advertising was headed "When the 1/1000th
counts", but no specification of its resolution has been found.[^sw8008] The distributor also
said that a few accessories adapted the console for cycling, skiing, equestrian events and
track.[^sw7709]

## Connectivity

This section covers the console's connections as the advertising describes them.

The deck wiring was a single-cord harness, which Seagull advertised as the only one of its
kind.[^sw7701] The starting pistol plugs straight into the console on a 15 ft cable, with an
optional 175 ft extension, and its transducer starts the timing when the shot is fired.[^sw7809]
The console drives scoreboards of one to ten lines. One-line boards step through the lanes in
turn, and multi-line boards could also show water polo and diving.[^sw7809][^sw7911] For relays,
the console compares each lane's touchpad with a relay judging unit on top of the block and
marks an early take-off on the printout. Seagull called this "An Omega Exclusive".[^sw7809]

[Splash Meet Manager](../../../software/splash-meet-manager.md) lists the OSM 5 among the
consoles it reads, over a one-way serial link.[^splashfaq]

## Documentation status

This section records what is and is not known.

No OSM-5 manual, data sheet or patent naming the console has been found. Its dimensions,
display, printer, resolution and output format are therefore undocumented here, and this page
has no specifications table. The 1976 systems shipped with operation and maintenance
manuals.[^sw7601]
