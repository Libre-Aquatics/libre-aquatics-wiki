The OCP5 is the touchpad sold under the Omega name by [Swiss Timing](../../../vendors/swiss-timing.md).
It is a stainless steel frame carrying a row of interlinked PVC slats, behind which tape-switch
contact strips close when a swimmer presses the face.[^manual][^ds] The model name appears in
Omega advertising by 1976, and the same name covers the four sizes in Swiss Timing's 2015
manual and datasheet.[^sw7601][^manual][^ds] Only the largest size, 240 × 90 cm, meets the World
Aquatics minimum for a touch panel, and Swiss Timing marks it as the FINA model.[^manual][^frules]

## Role in the timing system

This section places the OCP5 in an Omega installation; the general background on touchpads is
on the [touchpads overview](index.md).

The pad hangs on the end wall of each lane and signals the timer when a swimmer finishes or
turns. In a current Swiss Timing installation it plugs into the yellow socket of a lane module
on the [Quantum Mobile Harness](../timers/quantum-mobile-harness.md), or into an in-deck plate
wired to an [ODB10-SW](../timers/odb10-sw.md) box, and from there into a
[Quantum Aquatics](../timers/quantum.md) timer.[^harness][^swconcept] Earlier Omega pads worked
with the [OSM-5](../timers/osm-5.md) and [OSM6](../timers/osm6.md) consoles.[^sw7601][^sw8511]

## History

This section traces the pad from Omega's early advertising to the current Swiss Timing product.

Omega was advertising its swimming touchpads in the United States by 1973. Its exclusive North
American representative then said the pads had been in use since 1965 and did not leak,
corrode, warp or dent.[^sw7311] Omega's own history gives 1967 as the year it introduced
touchpads.[^omega] The [touchpads overview](index.md#history-and-development) covers the
conflicting dates for touch panels at the Games of that decade.

The OCP-5 name first appears in late 1975 and January 1976, in advertisements for Omega's
OSM-5, a portable printing timer sold as a complete touchpad system. Six-, eight- and ten-lane
packages cost $5,500, $6,300 and $7,800 and included OCP-5 pads measuring 5 ft by 2 ft, with
"standard sized" Omega pads of 6 ft 6 in by 2 ft as an option.[^sw7510][^sw7601] Omega's
advertising in 1977 and 1978 argued that the pad was what set the system apart, because
age-group swimmers touch more lightly than older ones.[^sw7708] In 1979 Omega described the
OCP-5 as a lightweight PVC pad of patented design that could be repaired on site and had seen
Olympic use.[^sw7911]

In 1985 Omega's US agent announced that FINA had allowed the equipment used at the 1984 Games,
OCP5 pads included, to be called "FINA Approved".[^sw8511] An equipment survey in 1991 described
the OCP5 as light and durable, responsive to even the lightest touch from a young swimmer and
unaffected by wave action.[^sw9108]

Omega patented two touch-pad designs in this period. The first, filed in 1971 with Swiss
priority from 1970, used a single perforated plastic plate.[^us275] The second, filed in 1976
by Omega's parent company SSIH, replaced the plate with separate hollow extruded panels hooked
to one another so that each could move a little on its own, over contact cables running across
them.[^us283] That second construction is the one the OCP5 manual describes, and the 1979
advertisement's "patented design" fits it, but no source names the patent and the product
together.[^us283][^manual][^sw7911]

The current manual was first issued in 2010 and revised to version 3.1 in February 2015.[^manual]
Its repair instructions still distinguish "old touchpads", whose slats hook together and whose
contact strips are glued in place, from current ones.[^manual]

## Design and construction

This section describes how the pad is built.

The frame is high-grade stainless steel. The face is made of individual PVC slats, linked side
by side and held at the top by a plastic sheath and snap-in fasteners. Yellow, black and
two-tone slats are available, and a set of yellow slats printed "Swiss Timing" can be fitted to
the 90 cm sizes.[^manual][^ds] Behind the slats, two or three tape-switch contact strips run
across the pad inside guide channels and protective sheaths, and are soldered in parallel to
the single cable. Polarity does not matter.[^manual]

Swiss Timing says the pad is unaffected by hard water and chemical corrosion, and that it can
be repaired by the owner on site without being sent back.[^manual][^ds]

## Contact operation and sensitivity

This section covers what makes the pad register a touch.

Pressure on any point of the face pushes a slat against the contact strips and closes them,
and the timer records the closure at once. A broad, even change in pressure, such as a wave,
does not close the strips, which is how the pad avoids false signals from wash.[^manual][^ds]

Swiss Timing's documents give three figures for the force needed:[^manual][^ds][^omega]

| Source | Trigger force |
|---|---|
| OCP5 manual, introduction | 2–3 kg of localised pressure |
| OCP5 manual, sensitivity test | 1.5–3.5 kp, measured with the supplied dynamometer |
| OCP5 datasheet | About 2 kg |
| Omega's swimming history page | 1.5–2.5 kg |

The test range is the most specific of these figures, because it is the value an installer
measures with the dynamometer.[^manual] Sensitivity can be reduced zone by zone by mounting a contact strip the other
way round, so that its large and small ribs swap places; Swiss Timing recommends leaving the
strips in their standard orientation.[^manual]

## Mounting

This section covers fixing the pad to the end wall.

The 60 cm pads hang from the gutter wall on angle brackets adjusted to the wall's thickness and
locked with a special nut. Swiss Timing uses this mounting for lanes narrower than 2.0 m.[^manual][^ds]
The 90 cm pads either sit on angle brackets fixed into 8 mm holes 65 mm deep, or are screwed
straight to the wall through 6 mm holes 45 mm deep; either way the pad must lie flat against the
wall.[^manual] Swiss Timing's installation guide sets the starting block 410 mm back from the
pool edge when an OCP5 is fitted, instead of the usual 400 mm.[^swconcept]

## Connection to the timer

This section covers how the pad's signal reaches the timer.

Each pad has one cable ending in a dual banana plug.[^manual][^harness] On a Quantum
installation it goes into the yellow socket of the lane's harness module or deck plate; the
white and red sockets beside it take the starting block and pushbuttons.[^harness][^swconcept]

## Care and maintenance

This section summarises Swiss Timing's care, test and repair instructions.

Swiss Timing advises taking pads out of the pool between sessions. After use they are rinsed with fresh
water, stood upright to drain, wiped, and left to dry for longer than they were in the water.
They are stored upright, on Swiss Timing's trolley where available, and never laid flat or
stacked, which can damage the contact strips.[^manual][^sw]

Before a meet, a buzzer on the pad's cable checks the pad. It should stay silent with no load,
and should sound when the dynamometer presses the middle of a slat at and below the water line.
The last check is a simulated touch into the timer.[^manual] A buzzer that sounds with no load
points to a convex pool wall, a bent strip or channel, debris behind a slat, or slats that have
come apart.[^manual]

Repairs are done on site. A contact strip that reads above 50 Ω when closed has oxidised
electrodes; one that reads around 100 kΩ when open has probably let water in. Either way the
strip is replaced. Single slats can also be swapped.[^manual]
