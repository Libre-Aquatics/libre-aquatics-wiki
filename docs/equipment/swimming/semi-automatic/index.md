Semi-automatic timing, or semi-automatic officiating equipment, is a swim timing
system that starts automatically from the starting device and is stopped by hand at
the finish, by officials pressing buttons or by timers stopping watches that the
start signal set running together. It sits between fully automatic timing, where a
[touchpad](../touchpad/index.md) records the finish without human involvement, and
manual timing, where a timer both starts and stops a watch. Because the start is
electronic and only the finish is human, a semi-automatic time carries the timer's
reaction delay at one end but not the other, which is why the rulebooks rank it below
automatic timing and above watches.

## History and the move from watches

Semi-automatic timing is older than the touchpad. Omega's Swim Eight-O-Matic, used at
the 1956 Melbourne Olympic Games, is described by the company as the first
semi-automatic swimming timer: its electronic counters were started by the starting
pistol and stopped at the finish by officials holding electric pushbuttons.[^omega1956]
Timing by hand at both ends was the alternative, so moving the start onto the starting
device removed one of the two human reactions from every result.

The official report of the 1960 Rome Olympic Games describes the method in operation.
Swimming used thirty timekeepers for the pool's ten lanes, and each lane also carried an
apparatus connected to the starter's pistol, with the finishes recorded by the
timekeepers pressing special buttons. Three times were taken per lane on the Omega
Swim-O-Matic, each button worked by a timekeeper independent of the other two assigned to
that lane, and the hand timekeeping service ran alongside the automatic system for
finishes and for intermediate times.[^rome60] An electronic start with three independent
human finishes per lane, checked against watches, is the arrangement the rulebooks still
describe.

The touchpad displaced the button from the primary role rather than retiring it, and it
arrived earlier than the vendor accounts suggest. The official report of the
1964 Tokyo Games records a fully automatic finish-order apparatus, triggered from the
starter's pistol and reading the finish from a then-new touch board, which the Games made
their official timer that year.[^tokyo64] The Munich 1972 report traces its own contact
mats back to those Tokyo pads and marks one change over the intervening eight years: by
1972 the sensing was electronic, where Tokyo's boards had still been
pneumatic.[^munich72] World Aquatics, writing
about its Omega partnership, instead dates the touch panel to the 1967 Pan American Games
and the 1968 Mexico City Olympic Games.[^waomega] The organising-committee reports are
the more authoritative source for what was installed at their own Games, and the Munich
note about pneumatic boards suggests the later date belongs to the electronic pad rather
than to touch boards as such. Once the swimmer's own touch could stop the clock, buttons
became the layer behind the pad, and the rulebooks were written around that arrangement:
automatic equipment first, semi-automatic behind it, watches behind that.

## How semi-automatic timing works

The [start system](../starter/index.md) sends its start pulse to the timing equipment at
the same instant it sounds the tone and flashes the strobe, so every lane's clock begins
with the signal. What differs between the two forms of semi-automatic timing is where
that clock runs. With buttons, the clock is in the
[timing console](../timers/index.md) and each lane's timer presses a button as the
swimmer touches, stopping that lane's clock. With a wireless stopwatch system, the clock
is in each timer's watch and the start signal starts every watch together, after which
the timer stops the watch by hand.

Either way the finish is a human judgment, so a lane usually carries more than one, and
the rules resolve the disagreement between them rather than trusting a single press.
Buttons are designated A, B, and C, each read independently of the pad.[^tdp] A press
records the timer's reaction to the touch rather than the touch itself, which is why a
semi-automatic time is treated as a check on a pad time and not as its equal.[^watiming]

That reaction delay falls at one end of the race only, and the asymmetry is large enough
that a console may correct for it. Colorado Time Systems' System 5 software applies a
0.15 second correction whenever the two methods are mixed: a manual start has 0.15
seconds added to the recorded start time, and a manual finish, which is the
semi-automatic case, has 0.15 seconds subtracted from the recorded time. CTS describes
0.15 seconds as the statistical median delay for manual start and finish timing and
attributes the figure to two studies by J. Killpatrick, Timing Accuracies for Swimming
(1975) and Timing and Judging Accuracies for Swimming (1976).[^f506] Where both the start
and the finish are taken by hand, which is manual timing, the manual states that no
correction is needed, because the late start compensates for the late
finish.[^f506]

CTS calls the two lane arrangements a touchpad finish and a button finish, and a
facility can run either. On a button finish the buttons plug into the same lane harness
connectors the pads would use, and with two buttons on a lane the console records the
average of the two times.[^f506]

## Governing-body requirements

Each governing body defines semi-automatic timing in its own rulebook, and the
definitions match closely even where the procedures around them differ. World Aquatics
defines the class by where the timer stops: for both automatic and semi-automatic
equipment the timer is started manually by the starter, and under semi-automatic
equipment either the touch panel stops it and the buttons are the backup, or, where
there are no working touch panels,
timekeepers pressing buttons stop the official timer themselves.[^wacr] The equipment
must be activated by the starter, be accurate to a hundredth of a second, and not
interfere with starts, turns, or the overflow system.[^wacr]

How many buttons a lane needs has changed. Under the Facilities Rules 2021–2025, a major
event could back its automatic equipment with semi-automatic equipment provided each lane
carried three buttons under three different officials, one of whom might be that lane's
inspector of turns.[^frules] The Competition Regulations in force from
February 2026 carry the same provision with one button per lane rather than
three.[^wacr] For records, a performance must be recorded by automatic equipment, or by
semi-automatic equipment where the automatic system malfunctioned.[^wacr][^watr] Where a
swimmer has no automatic time, the official time is the one recorded by semi-automatic
equipment or by timekeepers.[^wacr][^watr]

The two classes are started the same way and differ at the finish.

| | Automatic officiating equipment | Semi-automatic officiating equipment |
|---|---|---|
| Started by | the starter | the starter |
| Finish recorded by | the swimmer's touch on the panel | an official pressing a button |
| Buttons per lane as backup | not applicable | three (Facilities Rules 2021–2025), one (Competition Regulations from February 2026) |

USA Swimming states the hierarchy directly, in the article numbered 102.23 since the 2023
rulebook and 102.24 before it, a renumbering that still catches out officials' training
material written against the older number.[^usas][^renumber] Its rules rank the three in a
preferred order of use. An automatic system takes its start from the starting device and
its finish from the swimmer's own touch on the pad. A semi-automatic system starts the
same way, but the finish comes from timers pressing buttons as the swimmer arrives. A
manual system leaves both ends to the timer, on individual lane watches.[^usas] A meet then
designates its systems at three levels, and the rules fix what may serve at each.

| Level | What may serve, in preferred order | When it is required |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | automatic; semi-automatic with three or two buttons per lane; manual with three watches per lane | always |
| Secondary | integrated overhead video at 100 images per second; semi-automatic with one, two, or three buttons; manual with one to three watches | unless the primary system is watches |
| Tertiary | at least one manual watch per lane | unless the primary is watches, or the secondary is integrated video or already includes a watch per lane |

Each button or watch is operated by a separate timer, and secondary and tertiary times
are recorded but not used except to corroborate or correct the primary
result.[^usas] Where a lane produces more than one button or watch time, they resolve
into a single time for that system.

| Valid times available on a lane | Time for that timing system |
|---|---|
| Three, two of which agree | the agreeing time |
| Three, all disagreeing | the intermediate time |
| Two | the average, with thousandths dropped and not rounded |
| One | that time, unless it conflicts with other information |

The NCAA rule matches this in substance, and adds that a result may not blend the
secondary and tertiary tiers.[^ncaa] A difference of approximately 0.30 second or more
between the primary and backup times is one of the signs of a primary-system
malfunction.[^usas][^swimswam] One timer may hold two devices only where they differ, a
watch and a button rather than a pair of either.[^usas]

The NCAA classifies the same three tiers under its own numbering. On its definition a
semiautomatic device may be started either from the starter's signaling device or by hand,
and is stopped by one or more officials on a button switch. Championship meets must field
all three levels, which the NCAA glosses as primary equals automatic, secondary equals
semiautomatic, tertiary equals manual watch.[^ncaa] Its
ranking does not place semi-automatic above manual across the board: two watches per lane
outrank a single button.

| Priority level | Equipment |
|---|---|
| 1 | Automatic |
| 2 | Two-button semiautomatic |
| 3 | Manual, two watches to a lane |
| 4 | One-button semiautomatic |
| 5 | Manual, a single watch to a lane |
| 6 | One or more alternate watches covering the field; at championship meets, one per lane at minimum |

A time entered for an NCAA championship must be achieved on level one equipment. If the
head judge confirms a malfunction there, level two is acceptable, and if level two also
malfunctions, level three; no other level is accepted for championship
qualification.[^ncaa]

The NFHS defines a semiautomatic timing device as one that starts manually or
automatically and stops automatically or manually respectively, calibrated like every
other device to a hundredth of a second.[^nfhs] It names one automatic configuration and
two backup classes.

| Role | Equipment |
|---|---|
| Automatic timing | uniform electronic start with a touchpad finish |
| Backup, semi-automatic | uniform electronic start with timer-operated buttons at the finish |
| Backup, manual | watches started and stopped by timers |

Even when automatic timing is used, the NFHS requires a manual timing device for each
lane as backup, and its current guidance keeps semi-automatic times ahead of manual ones
when a final time has to be established.[^nfhs][^nfhsbp] Because the thresholds and
procedures differ between bodies, a meet follows the rules of its sanctioning
organization.

## Products

This section catalogs the semi-automatic timing equipment this wiki covers, in the two
forms the rules recognize: wired buttons and wireless stopwatches.

[Pushbuttons](pushbutton/index.md) are the traditional form, a hand-held button per timer
wired back to the timing console through a deck plate or cable harness.
[Colorado Time Systems](../../../vendors/colorado-time-systems.md) (CTS) and
[Daktronics](../../../vendors/daktronics.md) each make one, and Daktronics states that
the two makers' buttons are cross-compatible.[^dakcompat]

| Product | Manufacturer | Form | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Colorado Time Systems Pushbutton](pushbutton/cts.md) | Colorado Time Systems | Wired button (`PB-6`) | Current |
| [Daktronics Pushbutton](pushbutton/daktronics.md) | Daktronics | Wired button | Supplied with deck-cabling systems |

The [Dolphin Wireless Stopwatch Timing System](dolphin.md) is the wireless form, in which
each lane timer holds a stopwatch instead of a plugged-in button and the
[Dolphin Starter Unit](../starter/dolphin-starter-unit.md) starts every watch together
from the start signal. USA Swimming has allowed wireless systems of this kind to serve as
semi-automatic timing in sanctioned competition since April 13, 2023.[^blog]

| Product | Manufacturer | Form | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Dolphin Wireless Stopwatch Timing System](dolphin.md) | Colorado Time Systems | Wireless stopwatches, synchronized start | Current |

Each article covers its equipment in full, how a timer uses it, how it connects, and its
care; this page is the shared overview they refer back to.

Other manufacturers sell semi-automatic equipment this wiki has not yet documented.
[International Sports Timing](../../../vendors/international-sports-timing.md) offers
wired buttons as an alternative to touchpads for facilities on smaller budgets, with two
buttons per lane in place of a pad.[^ist] ALGE-Timing sells its semi-automatic package in
both a cabled form and a radio form, each covering eight lanes plus the start
channel.[^alge] Time Drops and Wylas Timing are newer entrants whose wireless buttons and
phone-based watches take the start through an adapter cabled to an existing electronic
start system.[^timedrops][^wylas]
