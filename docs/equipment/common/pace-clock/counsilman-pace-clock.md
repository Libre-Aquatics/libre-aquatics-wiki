The Counsilman Pace Clock was a large sweep-hand clock for swim training that James "Doc"
Counsilman, the Indiana University swimming coach, sold by mail from Bloomington, Indiana. It was
advertised in Swimming World from October 1964, when it was already described as an improved
model, until August 1971. From 1967 it was sold with a smaller battery model, the Porta-Pace
Clock.[^sw6410][^sw6701][^sw7108]

## Role in training

A pace clock lets swimmers read their own send-off and repeat times; see the
[pace clocks overview](index.md) for the history of the class. Counsilman's advertisements named
three uses for the clock: holding spectators' interest, pacing distance swimmers, and giving a
basis for interval training.[^sw6410] In March 1964 a column of the American Swimming Coaches
Association noted that Counsilman, Peter Daland and George Haines all trained their swimmers with a
large pacing clock, placed at an end of the pool or at each end, so that the coach no longer had to
time every swimmer with a stopwatch.[^asca64]

## Origins

The earliest dated record of the clock found is the October 1964 advertisement. It called the clock
an improved model and compared its motor with the one in earlier models, so earlier versions
existed, but none was found in print.[^sw6410]

Later accounts date Counsilman's first pace clock much earlier, and they disagree with each other.
A 1994 Swimming World tribute dates both his introduction of interval training and his design of
a clock to suit it to 1949, and credits him with building thousands of the first clocks made for
that kind of training.[^sw9411] A Swim News profile of 2004 puts it a year earlier, in 1948,
and calls him the first maker of such clocks.[^swimnews04] Neither account
cites a product record. Australian sources credit an earlier clock to Forbes Carlile and Frank
Cotton; the [pace clocks overview](index.md) sets out those claims.

## Design and models

This section describes the clocks as Counsilman's advertisements present them.

The main clock was an electric dial clock with a minute hand and a sweep second hand, both set by
hand. The 1964 model had a face that the advertisement called easier to read, a 60 Hz motor said to
be three times as strong as the one used before, and a switch for starting and stopping it.[^sw6410] From late 1965 the hands were balanced and the clock came with wall brackets and a
swivel leg for standing it on the deck, which also made it easier to store.[^sw6511] The 1967
advertisement gives the face as 3 ft square and asks buyers to say whether they wanted the wall,
deck or combined version.[^sw6701]

The Porta-Pace Clock, added in 1967, was a portable clock in a plexiglass case with a 12 in square
face, weighing under 5 lb. Its minute and second hands were driven by a jewelled movement made in
Germany and powered by one ordinary flashlight battery, which the advertisement said would last a
year. It was meant for a single swimmer or a small group.[^sw6701]

## Sales and pricing

The clocks were sold by mail order, with Counsilman paying the shipping by rail express, and were
advertised alongside his stroke-analysis and starts-and-turns films.[^sw6410][^sw6511] By November
1965 the advertisement claimed more than 1,000 clocks sold.[^sw6511]

| Period | Pace clock | Porta-Pace | Order address (Bloomington, Indiana) |
|---|---|---|---|
| October 1964 – 1966 | $65.00 | Not offered | 1917 Marilyn Drive[^sw6410] |
| January 1967 – February 1969 | $65.00 | $40.00 | 1917 Marilyn Drive[^sw6701] |
| March – May 1969 | $65.00 | $40.00 | 208 N. Washington Street[^sw6903] |
| June 1969 – February 1970 | $65.00 | $40.00 | R.R. 2, Heritage Woods Road[^sw6906] |
| March – July 1970 | $65.00 | $40.00 | R.R. 12, Heritage Woods Road[^sw7003] |
| August 1970 – August 1971 | $75.00 | $45.00 (by October 1970) | R.R. 12, Heritage Woods Road[^sw7008][^sw7010] |

The last advertisement found is from August 1971.[^sw7108] From December 1972 Counsilman's films
were sold by the [Counsilman Company](../../../vendors/counsilman-company.md), at first from the
same rural-route address, which also sold isokinetic exercisers and books. None of its
advertisements found offers a pace clock.[^sw7212][^sw7406]

None of the advertisements names the firm that built the clocks, and no patent for a pace clock in
Counsilman's name has been found.

## Later recognition

In its review of the twentieth century, Swimming World in January 2001 chose Counsilman as the
person who had given most to swimming in that century, and listed the pace clock among the five innovations
swimmers most take for granted, without naming an inventor.[^sw0101]
